There is a brief patch of sunlight between the metropolitan stations of the specific Shanghai metro line—line 2—that I know better than half my friends. 

I’ve never relied on public transport to get to a place—my family owned a car and in my youth I lived right next to my school, within walkable distance. 

The first time I saw the sunlight I was eight, and my Aunt had taken me to be a helper at her English agency. I was somewhat proficient at English by that time—enough to be considered more a helper than a burden—and we took the train into the city in the early mornings. She enticed me by taking me to lunch out every time, and I obsessed over her at that time because she was young, and cool, and didn’t discipline me as my mother did. The metro with her was the first time I had experienced rush hour traffic on the metro, but also the first time, amidst the faint scent of bodies in the sweltering summer and the stale, cold air conditioning on full blast, that I was surrounded by light after such a long stretch of darkness. I could hardly open my eyes. I was tired, I was unsure, and I was but a child. But in that moment I was drenched in sunlight.

Now, an aboveground train is scarcely a rare occurrence. Even on that same route, the train rose above ground in the easternmost section, the section that reached all the way to Pudong airport. But this brief excursion aboveground occurs for not even a minute, perhaps only forty seconds. The train lifts itself out of the ground, drives briefly there, then sinks gradually again into the underground network. That, however, was a later revelation that the child in that moment was wholly oblivious to.

Six years later, I was in Stony Brook. It was a Saturday and I was at the local coffee shop, looking over a hard SAT reading problem. It was about trains, and how some station in London—King’s Cross, in particular—symbolized freedom, symbolized the untouched wilds and world beyond in some literary work. Eager for validation and even more eager to get the SAT over with, I could not care less usually about these esoteric, redundantly worded comprehension questions.

It was beating a dead horse, but was it?

I thought back on all the times I’ve ridden that train, briefly endowed by a half minute of beautiful light on a good day, the eight-car boxes billowing left and right on the tracks ascending as if resurrected by some unseen hand, the passengers swaying along in silence with it. It sure as hell symbolized freedom in my life. Westward I rode it; I lived in the less-developed east, and it took me beyond into the city, into all the colors and lights, all the tastes and smells of the metropolitan. And I only then realized how much I had become attached to that feeling, that I was wading into a city that was alive, a city that I belonged in. A city that took all of me.

All set off by thirty seconds of unannounced sunlight on the westward train. 

And I still think of it now when the LIRR rolls slowly out of Smithtown towards Huntington. I think of it when I cycle westwards in Shanghai (for cardio or for leisure), and I see the first lights of skyscrapers and street-food vendors starting to emanate in each new corner, keeping me company and taking the place lonely concrete freeways and endless bypasses of Suburban Shanghai. I think of that one particular instnace sunlight and wonder if that it really meant companionship, meant community, meant my own human agency when I take slow, long walks in the suburban woods on Long Island.
