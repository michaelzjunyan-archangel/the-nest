Oh, to write about this masterpiece is like getting “Ideal Gas Law” on your charades card. I don’t even know where to start. As a book that took itself up in the “romance novel” and “ easy classics” of my bookshelf, I spent a whopping four months in total finishing it. You really couldn’t blame me, because the book spans 418,053 words cover to cover, or about five-and-a-half regular, 80K word length novels. It is the first narrative of such expansive scope and detail, which gives it the title “Best love story of all time.” I will disagree with that later.



To add to this, the book is split into distinct parts and arcs—roughly demarcated by each of Scarlett’s marriages—-and the story towards the end is hardly the story we start off with. With a novel of such magnitude, the level of detail in the side characters and background is beyond immaculate and, though it often leads to me guiltily skipping lines which delineate the affairs of the Fontaines or Munroes, I could hardly imagine squeezing this amount of detail into a movie (even a 4-hour one, at that)



What other authors imply or show with a scene Mitchell dedicates paragraphs to. It is as much a chronicle, a commentary, and an encyclopedia of Southern life in the years immediately preceding and following the Civil War as it is a novel set in that time period. We are given one of the most beautiful, detailed expositions known to literary history, and it really fooled me into believing I was reading a sophisticated, detailed, yet lighthearted romance. The slow, beautiful glamour of the pre-war South, the orderliness and abundance in which Scarlett and company inhabited could not have been more vividly painted. 



The narrative takes a hard turn after the war starts, and we spend winters in nervous anticipation, entrapped emotionally within Scarlett’s first short-lived marriage. We really don’t get that many new characters that’re important after this part, which makes the suspense and twists Mitchell manages even more astonishing. I could not wrap my head around how the story might end until it ended (and I don’t like how it ended, but we’ll get to that.) If anything, Mitchell really likes making us wait after she drops an idea. I was wondering about Ashley, Rhett, and how she was going to return to well-to-do after she becomes a starving, poor girl with not even shoes. But we don’t find out for the next 400 pages. 



If there’s one thing Mitchell spends the most time on, it’s detailing Scarlett’s inner turmoil. We learn, before there are any real challenges to prove it, that Scarlett is different from her peers of the Southern aristocracy. She has life, and vigor, and a certain obstinacy she got from her Irish roots. Throughout the story, she never stops fighting something—anything that gets in the way of her goals. But she doesn’t do it easily, and especially her safety nets are stripped from her, one at a time. She stays cold and pragmatic, calculating and ruthless for most of the time she’s impoverished. She puts a price on her body, her moral standards, and everything else. As the head of Tara, she becomes a source of near-divine providence for her peers by sacrificing everything she had if needed. Despite Scarlett’s notions of living up to Ellen, it’s not really a secret she’s never been a lady—in the sense that Ellen was a lady. She was an unscrupulous, opportunistic stallion spurred on only by her own interests, and she acted ladylike when she felt like it. Compare this Ellen’s sacrifice of her own health to aid her economically impoverished “white trash” neighbors, and you can begin to see the divide between moral integrity and unrelenting pragmatism. Her denial of her actions under the pretense of necessity creates a nook of self-deception where her core motivations and traits take shape; They do so gradually, slowly corrupting the pacified and still values that she followed in interest of gentlewomanhood. 



One quote that is used to describe Scarlett later strikes me as the most accurate: “You can’t put anything beyond her.” Her three marriages of convenience show increasing lengths she’d go to achieve her goals, but it isn’t enough to say that “She fell down the slippery slope with the first concession.” I believe that, had situations been dire enough, she would’ve been capable of doing anything the first time around. And her sheer vigor is beautiful, even more so in distress. She plays the damsel when she knows there is a knight, but more often she is the warrior taking on the world with any and all means. We are given hope—just enough to believe in a happy ending— and we have it taken away every time; we think the new Scarlett has it under control, that she finally gets what she wanted, but then disaster strikes again.



“he had never known such gallantry as the gallantry of Scarlett O'Hara going forth to conquer the world in her mother's velvet curtains and the tail feathers of a rooster.”



This pragmatism leads to conflictual, inflammatory confrontations with the fundamental core of Ashley—a Philosopher, a Dreamer. They’re opposites in virtually every way, and they never “got” each other. Saying its different morals doesn’t do it justice. Behaviorally, they pull in different directions to the exact same burden. The book makes that plenty clear, but our narrator Scarlett doesn’t see the problem. She had overextended herself to solve problems to such a degree, that there’s almost a grim (and awfully wrong) belief that affection can be forced if she was obstinate enough. 



“We've come a long way, both of us, since that day, haven't we, Scarlett? We've traveled roads we never expected to travel. You've come swiftly, directly, and I, slowly and reluctantly.”



And the most heartwrenching part about the book is how beautifully Mitchell goes full circle with her subtleties like Scarlett’s dreams, her habit of hiding trauma behind bulwarks of security or time itself, and Melanie’s ask of her to take care of her children on two separate occasions. The last one I especially felt. When Scarlett remarked that she’d much rather Melanie dead instead of being a burden that desolate day during the siege of Atlanta, I was wholeheartedly on her side. Although ethically indecent, I thought Mitchell must’ve drafted some way for the two to end up together anyhow. Moreover, we had been given a narrative (by Scarlett) that Melanie was simple, unopinionated, and undeserving of Ashley’s love—or her help. The conflict had also been scarily simple—get Ashley, and live happily ever after.



Because of this earlier depiction, the later chapters painting Melanie’s unyielding loyalty, her unconditional trust, and her kindness becomes especially poignant. Every insult, every tipped remark only adds to her characterization from an “obstacle” to “family.” It is especially intriguing to see her choose her actions despite having power, in a sense, over Scarlett in the Reconstruction. Mitchell was not murky with the power dynamics and hierarchy of respect—between plantation owners and regular farmers, between the new government and Northerners (republicans) and the South, between slaves and so-called free negroes. But, as the story later reveals, there is a fundamental distinction between economic success and social status especially in such a bifurcated society. Scarlett chose to cut herself off from the “Old Guard” and her old status which came with adherence to strict customs; Melanie chose to uphold that honor in pride even in hardship (Reverence for the Confederacy, honor to the veterans;  

 loyalty to old forms, pride in poverty, open hands to friends, and undying hatred to Yankees, specifically,) and that old society rewarded her for it. I wouldn’t blame her for pursuing money and security exclusively—the forms were aristocratic, sure, but the forms were all that was left of their shattered society. It was not a matter of personal shortfall, but societal collapse; A complete reshuffling of the cards. In that new, strange world, names of old and names that evoked honor seemed nothing more than cruel disillusionment that an entire nation refuses to accept.



But, I will say she falls into the fault of envy, ostentation, and bitterness from her idolizing of money, even befriending the perpetrators and the “enemy.” 



“Oh—a diamond ring—and Rhett, do buy a great big one." 

"So you can flaunt it before your poverty-stricken friends and say 'See what I caught!'  

Very well, you shall have a big one, one so big that your less-fortunate friends can comfort themselves by whispering that it's really vulgar to wear such large stones.”



This quote captures what I mean, I suppose, and the sad thing is that her opulent lifestyle after marrying Rhett doesn't really make her a great deal happier. 





To call Scarlett a martyr of her bygone aristocracy would be neglecting that, since the start of the Civil War, the disintegration of her generational wealth was somewhat inevitable. She had actively chosen not to die with it, to marry, plow, and even prostitute her way back into aristocracy and abundance economically, only to find that the “Old Guard,” only now slowly getting back on their feet, have cast her out utterly for her ruthless pragmatism and refusal to abide by social customs that disadvantaged women.



This dissolving, rebuilding, and rearranging of power structures parallel to social status is the most fascinating aspect of it as a novel. Alongside interpersonal relationships, we see each character’s interplay and their role, especially the old aristocracy. Characters like Mammy and Big Sam, who remain loyal and contemptuous of their own kind in slavery; Union sympathizers 

And “white trash” exalted to wealth for betraying their own state; Each character holds a distinct perspective on who has power, and who deserves to have power.



But, despite undermining her own legitimacy, Melanie unconditionally supported Scarlett in her actions. She did so at the cost of half of her social circle and direct kin. It hurts to want to root for Scarlett and Ashley, yet realize how vulnerably, gallantly oblivious Melanie is all the way till the end. 



Most of the story is told through Scarlett’s perspective and we follow her closely, but towards the end the narrative abruptly zooms out and becomes more jumpy. We get the conclusion of a scene with a promised action (ex. Rhett promises to buy her a ring) and the next paragraph immediately skips to the consequences following the action. Months and years are omitted, and the last chapter or so skips directly to the scenes with the most emotional caliber through (questionable) plot developments, which felt surprising given how meticulous each moment was described in the exposition and early days. 



That’s really my problem with the ending. For 400K words, the final scenes simply don’t match the earlier, elaborate characterizations. We hastily see the birth and death of a child that completely changes Rhett, the death of Melanie, a complete reversal of Scarlett’s affection towards Ashley, and a failed confession with Rhett. The novel reaches no resounding conclusion; No pistol is fired and no lost love is found. Mitchell seems to be suggesting that their feelings were simply untimely—mismatched—and downplaying her own buildup in the way. Sure, it’s justified for Scarlett and Ashley to suddenly feel distant—the drama, the aging and realization of nostalgia, the death of Melanie, their shared confidante that happened to also be their victim of this messy entanglement among others are all worthy reasons. But Mitchell delivers it in a way that tastes like:



“Oh, I realized he didn’t love me and I didn’t love him back. I was just delusional for the past twelve years. I loved a version that wasn’t true. All the while I let down my true love while he desperately loved me. Now I have nothing. ” 



That works for a romance. Not this one.
