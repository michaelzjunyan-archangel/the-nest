my ardent defense for the latesummer

Albert Camus writes in Return to Tipasa, “In the depths of winter, I finally learned that within me there lay an invincible summer.” A part of the larger collection Summer, the essay is written in the wider context of the post-WWII landscape, political disillusionment, and a journey of finding fulfillment in life. Earlier in the essay about his return to Tipasa, Camus “hoped…to recapture there a freedom I could not forget.” My defense for latesummer is that, in it, we may hope to find the freedom that keeps us yearning.



And it’s a great quote in its own right. We have taken it, out of context ad nauseam, to celebrate the sentiment of an irrepressible human triumph against the frosts of life, the bleak midwinter. And, to the great dismay of snow-day zealots, Christmas sympathizers, or fans of winter in general, the season has become analogous in symbolism for inactivity, difficulty, and death. In all fairness, the leisurely aspects of winter were only enjoyed rather recently—after food security and indoor heating became less of a luxury and more of a given. The cold, in itself, is a lack of energy because particles that make up everything move slower. It’s no wonder it carries a similar connotation.



Still, as nature hands us these hurdles, we have been concurrently getting increasingly deft at jumping them. As a modern Homo Sapiens living in the non-equatorial first world, all four seasons are now equally valid choices to endorse. We have even expanded our reasoning to beyond the seasons itself, but rather the imagery following it. Ask any Autumn fan what they like most, and you will not be able to avoid the warm, golden images of pumpkins, leaves, books and warm coffee. Followers of Spring pull out the trite ideas of life, birth, fertility, and just being out of winter in general (Another stab at the winter folks, sorry.) There’s a reason we have groundhog day to try and foretell the spring, but no skyhog day (or something to equivalent effect) to try and welcome the winter.



But, almost as infamous as winter is another season, that being the latesummer. If winter can be characterized as the end of everything good and, in all respects, a hiatus until the beautiful cycles of life start anew, then the late summer is the vague prequel to winter, the beginning of the end of the year. As a kid, one suffers in the disillusionment that their summer has ended, and now there is no choice but to reluctantly let the busier routines of school, autumn, and the march towards eventual adulthood wash over oneself. Freedom shirks in the face of the societally demanded responsibility that, for some reason, kids are given an unfathomably long reprieve from in the summer months specifically—a gift that feels like a curse by the end. 



Adults, while not bound by a physical shift in rhythm so abrupt, so worthy of shock and agonizing, are also not invulnerable to the latesummer melancholy. The evening starts being a little longer than necessary, stores come out with their ostentatious fall-themed drinks, clothes, and other paraphernalia, and the year starts to feel like its ending, however many months there are left. 



Thing is, summer is this expansive, infinite entity. It sweeps and holds us in its ever-longer days, it represents the peak of organic life—textbooks always show a tree as a sprout in the spring, its fullest in the summer, abscising, golden in the fall, and withered by winter—summer is the man not yet of middle ages, but has long since shed the childishness of youth, where physical capacities reconcile themselves with slow-coming mental ones of patience, reason, and wisdom (thank you, prefrontal cortex, for not developing fully until we’re halfway in the dirt)



Even a summer sunset is a rare occurrence. It happens quietly, usually after one is already resigned to the activities of the evening indoors. A winter sunset, on the other hand, happens eagerly before I even walk out of the STEM lab at 6:15. (If you have ever tried getting to Kanas in the dark, after two hours of robotics, maybe you can see why I am a staunch opposer of winter) A summer sunset prompts you to wait, to ponder, to exist in its fleeting orange glow for hours, while the winter sunset ushers in an impatient dark. 



But latesummer is a different animal. It only occurs when summer is exhausted enough—enough for even the most procrastinating of perceivers to realize that, wow, summer is almost over. As soon as that gut-wrenching, slightly unnerving realization happens, latesummer begins. If you want a textbook definition, it usually begins mid-August and lasts until late September, when the Autumnal Equinox happens. At face value, its indicators suggest that it's positively awful. Like a Sunday night before a busy week(s). And, again, an explanation could easily be found empirically. The latesummer demarcates something that not even the bleak winter portends: The midpoint of the year, the start of a slow, staggering decline into winter, inactivity, and the cold that winter \*purportedly\* holds. 



But the worst part about latesummer is also its most beautiful trait—the awareness that a period of time—good time—is ending, becoming emotionally vivid as it is almost gone and in the process of doing so. Summer is uncomfortably warm, sometimes lethargic, and perennially wet; We still can’t help but start looking through our bucket lists as it ends. Though, even as it stands, modern humans are still not creatures who can afford to spend all winter (or all summer, you winter lovers) sulking in somberness and melancholy—we still have things to get done, good memories to make, and a life largely heedless to weather that we need to live. 



But the thought in the back of our heads, the vague sentiment of the shifting seasons, both physically and metaphorically (People, for some reason, find it oddly appealing to sync their resolutions with the change of the seasons, referred to likewise \*winter arc\* \*spring lock-in,\*) is just significant enough that we begin to inhabit this transient verge, this liminal space between the bygone and the hereafter, where we feel more. Sudden awareness elevates our senses and hastens our search for meaning, but cannot delay an inevitable. A walk down to the park, a day with a friend, being out in the city—they start feeling a little less like what they actually are and a little more magical. A little more like “the” thing.



And that is my defense, my PSA, my plug for the latesummer. It’s good because it's at the end. And we have a nasty habit of not remembering to cherish things until they’re gone.
