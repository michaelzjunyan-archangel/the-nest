The Correspondent

Virginia Evans

Junyan Zhang



Literary. adj., relating to, or having the characteristics of humane learning or literature.

The problem I had with the term literary fiction is that it’s like saying Chai tea. Chai is tea, it literally means tea. Literary fiction generally refers to serious, character-driven storytelling that prioritizes deep exploration of the human condition, social themes, and artistic prose over a strict commercial plot or genre formula. 



Okay, sure. But that sounds very boring. I was overwhelmed by a singular question, which Sybil restates in her letters to Rosalie in the Correspondent, “How does letters of an old lady mean anything to anybody except herself?” Further, how is this even an entire book?” I carried that question into the book and found it dissolved about three pages in.



That is to say, The Correspondent thoroughly proved me wrong. Following Sybil Stone Van Antwerp, a retired clerk for a judge, the book is an anthology of her correspondence (letters) with various people in the frame from 2012 to 2022, the last ten years of her life. The first “literary” thing about this premise I had to get over was that there was no “plot engine” in the traditional sense. The world does not change as a result of her, there’s no formulaic focus on romance or action, and she doesn’t really desire much physically, to be honest, for much of the book. She is exactly as you would expect an old lady to be, no offense to any old ladies actively saving the world.



The book is set so late in her life that most of the events the book centers around have happened before the fact, and she writes of them in hindsight only. In fact, so little changes on her own accord that it almost juxtaposes and accentuates the changes that she is forced to accept, the changes that she passively engages as the world changes around her. The shifting dynamics of other people’s lives, to which she is inadvertently or directly a recipient, is where most of the plot comes from.



Again, as a child fed on fantasy—high and low—this was thoroughly enlightening to me. To use no grand world, no rapid change in life, and yet still tangle the strings in the reader’s heart and pull them resoundingly with emotion…I have read fiction based on regular old people but never at such a high caliber. It teaches you the same thing good music teaches you—to make something emphatic was never about making it loud, or shoving in the most elements, but it was all about contrast and buildup. A good drop shares many things with a good climax in a book.



So, back to Sybil. She lives a quiet life alone as an affluent, retired, small woman with perhaps nothing of note except a habit of letter-writing and somewhat obstinate opinions. But these opinions reach only so far as power struggles within the gardening club and writing to the local newspaper. The text is minimally concerned with worldbuilding and really only has one main character that gets interiority developed—the others are all viewed through a lens, their internal activity solely dictated through the information they choose to divulge to our dear Sybil.



The book has a secondary level of depth in these discrepancies between trading correspondence and actual human interaction—which we learn through more letters after the fact, of course—making a unique, inadvertent metacommentary on the nature of human communication. We are shown what seems to be through the written communications between Sybil and the world, and then we learn the reality of those events. Evans suggests through the plot that, more often than not, we carry a version of ourselves and stuff that version into the envelope; our real selves are a lot more dynamic and there are things that simply never could be said over a letter. Some would say the book is about her life—I would dare to venture that the book is really about the human divides that shape her life. The human divide so strong that the book can derive meaning off of convincing us why something mattered when, by conventional standards, almost nothing happened at all. 





Sybil writes with vitriol and animosity towards the dean who has been denying her audits of classes and the Dean writes back, with equal force, rejections each time for years. But, when they confront one another face to face, they read the cues that had never, in all the correspondence shared, made its way across the lines of text. She speaks with her late husband and her late son, but these thoughts and unsent letters are characterized by remorse and will never develop, never entwine into a reciprocal conversation. She is so much into order and a sense of control characterized by her profession and her letters that, inevitably, her inability to reconcile with the haphazard, messy, yet tender nature of relationships threaten to swallow her whole.  We see her prejudice towards an online serviceperson—which then turns into a deep, transforming relationship; we see her claiming to be stubborn, then relenting to the kind requests of young students. You see her struggle to take a class because she wanted to, and how a heated struggle turned out to be a misunderstanding that showed she had more in common with her “enemies” than she thought. She claims to love the straw men she constructs in her head and the order it creates, but increasingly in her experiences she finds those assumptions contradicted.



But she keeps writing, and she says she is too old to change. Her tie to her original family hinges on one small letter, and perhaps that plays a part too. But even after time and again she is disproven, she sticks with her way of correspondence. Obstinacy, inertia, or trauma is my best guess.



Insofar as Sybil writes her letters, we learn equal parts what she wants to say but also who she is. How she chooses to see things. Again, as the “mailbox” we occupy a unique vantage point of observation where interiority sits awkwardly between close third person and first person, where we see her motives and emotions in the contrast of tone, diction, and complexity rather than action or explicit thought. In first person, we are offered an account directed toward the reader—and in third person the narrator draws us a picture up for interpretation through actions, relationships, and thoughts. Through epistolary fiction, though, we are really doing subtraction. We are trying to find what the author is trying to say about Sybil through the differences, by way of compare and contrast in an account directed to other characters. We have to put ourselves in multiple different pairs of shoes.



And I guess in the end, she doesn’t resolve anything. It’s not a sad story, per se, in the events that happen within the frame of the story. It is a story about a woman who lingered in sadness, and her struggle to stay afloat, to run away from that perpetual sadness and discontent, that vitriol into forgiveness and peace seen through the strokes of her pen. Neatly, the book starts with an obituary and ends with an obituary, and it’s almost as if we are rummaging through her belongings after the fact, never truly getting to know her most intimate emotions firsthand. If the book is sparked by the death of someone which represents the end of a long, winding era of her life, her own demise seems a lot less meaningful. It’s not a natural ending point where some big resolution is reached—Evans makes a point out of that by, spoilers alert, making her die in the midst of writing a letter—it’s much rather a gradual cessation of her human agency, a fading sound as the outro ends. Her human limit finally conquers her desire for control, and her death almost serves as an invitation for us to reflect, for us to dive back into her life now at a finish and ponder her choices for her.



I’ve started writing paper-and-pen letters to my friends this year. A friend who lives in Canada had, after pestering me for a lengthened period of time, convinced me to do so. Writing a letter is comfortable in a way like no other way of writing I partake in. I don’t have to worry about being detailed as I do in my journals, I don’t have to worry about accuracy or logic or sophistication, I don’t have to worry about the characterization and the whole slurry of precautions of a narrative writer. 



I just write whatever that I want to talk about. It’s been saving my life.



A work has quantifiable metrics attached to it. A letter doesn’t. It is an incomplete, sometimes even inaccurate performance of our own selves, our mental schema and in some ways a low level imitation of our entire consciousness hastily or idly scribbled onto paper. And when we look back, those things that weren’t propped up to be read as impressive, or detailed, or polished, are often the most accurate portrayals of ourselves.



And maybe they matter to nobody but ourselves. But somewhere in between Sybil’s letters and my own—somewhere between each of our individual understandings and matters we arbitrarily endowed with meaning—lies the human condition that tells us a pretty good story about who we are.
